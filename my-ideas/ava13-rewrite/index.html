  • The animation will primarily be done in Blender, and everything that can be automated (without losing any quality) will be. For example, the serrations on TDL's ViraBlades and various particle-effects and stylistic motion-blurs will be generated algorithmically, with carefully-constructed equations, and there will be sequences where many instances of H4CK3R multibox. Each effect will be drawn in 2D animation at least once (if it hasn't already been) first, and then algorithms with various parameters to replicate their appearance will be developed. (If you want, you can circumvent this by drawing as many unique/generalisably-single-use effects as possible.) This approach has several benefits:
    • Keyframes are continuously scalable and can be placed on exteger-frames
    • Animations can be programmed, simulated and broadly deployed, and then copied, transposed, rotated and edited in 3D, which will help when there are lots and lots of intricate and redundant elements
    • Elements can be kept at certain positions in 3D space, which means that cinematography and camera-angles can be altered and experimented with at will, and elements can remain in place over short and long scales. You don't usually hear about this but I often notice stuff just disappearing in the background between inconsistent shots, or even in the first moment when they apparently become choreographically irrelevant, and characters being AFK NPCs (hence my principle of focusing on each character separately)
    • Animations can be tweaked in 3D space, and not require entire redos for relatively minor changes
    • Shapes, proportions and systems can be more simply defined and maintained
    • It is more practically feasible to storyboard and animate heavily complex sequences with 3D positioning on a second pass
    • It will save time so we can maximise other aspects like the elements and details that can be added, and work on other things
